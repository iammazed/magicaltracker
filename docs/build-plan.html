Build plan · MagicalTracker.com · iOS

A passport for every trip you take

MagicalTracker is a Disney World trip log: where you ate, where you stayed, what you earned. This is the whole path from empty folder to App Store — the stack, the cuts, the schema, the paywall, and the six things most likely to sink it.

Target iOS 17+ Stack Expo + Supabase To launch ~11 weeks part-time Cost to launch ~$150

Read before anything else

Four things that change the plan

Your feature list is strong. But four realities reshape it before you write a line of code, and it's much cheaper to absorb them now than in month three.

1. You're on Windows, so Swift is off the table

Xcode is macOS-only, full stop. That isn't a preference — you physically cannot build a native SwiftUI app on Windows 11. This single fact settles the framework debate: you're using Expo (React Native), which builds in the cloud through EAS Build and needs no Mac at any point. Happily, that's also the stack that best matches what you already write.

You'll need a real iPhone for testing (the simulator is Mac-only) — that's fine, and honestly better, since you're building an app used while walking around a theme park.

2. There is no My Disney Experience API

Disney publishes no public API for itineraries, reservations, or wait times. The only way to read someone's MDE plans is to ask for their Disney credentials and scrape — which violates Disney's terms, creates a genuine security liability for you, and will get the app rejected under App Store guideline 5.2.

Cut this feature

Remove MDE integration from the roadmap entirely. Replace it with manual trip entry — the user types "Ohana, Thursday 7:40pm" — and get the same payoff ("you've eaten here before, you rated it 4★") from data you own. It's 2% of the engineering and 100% legal.

3. Disney's legal team is the real risk, not the code

Apps like TouringPlans, Thrill-Data, and a dozen dining trackers coexist with Disney fine, so this is workable — but only if you stay strictly inside nominative fair use: you may factually name a place ("Be Our Guest Restaurant, Magic Kingdom") because there's no other way to refer to it. You may not borrow the brand's look. Full breakdown in Risks, and the name "MagicalTracker" itself deserves an attorney's eye.

4. Half your premium features need users you don't have yet

Top 10 lists, trending restaurants, "people like you also ate at…", tracking other users on the map — every one of these is empty on launch day and stays empty until you have thousands of logged visits. If your paywall is built entirely on community data, day-one subscribers pay for blank screens and churn immediately.

The fix is sequencing: v1 premium sells personal depth (notes, photos, unlimited trips, full passport, widgets, export). v1.1 premium adds community once the data exists. Build the tables and the aggregation from day one so the feature switches on the moment there's something to show.

Why this wins

Roughly 80% of what you already know carries over, and EAS Update means a typo fix ships in ten minutes instead of a two-day App Store review. The trade is real but small: Live Activities and home-screen widgets need a native config plugin rather than a few lines of SwiftUI. Worth it.

How the pieces actually talk

// Catalog — read-mostly, rarely changes, must work offline
Supabase venues table
  └─ nightly export → versioned JSON bundled in the app build
       └─ seeded into expo-sqlite on first launch
            └─ delta sync on app open ("catalog_version" check)

// User data — writes often, must sync across devices
App write → expo-sqlite (instant, optimistic UI)
         → queued mutation → Supabase (RLS-scoped to auth.uid())
         → on reconnect, replay queue

// Aggregates — community ratings, Top 10, trending
Postgres materialized view, refreshed hourly by pg_cron
  └─ read by app, never computed on device

That split is the whole architecture. Catalog data flows down and is never written by users; user data flows up and is never read by others except through aggregates. Getting this boundary right on day one is what keeps the app fast in a dead zone behind Space Mountain.

Monetization

Free vs. Premium, and what to charge

One rule governs the split: never paywall data entry. Every rating a free user logs is an asset that makes your Top 10, your trending list, and your recommendation engine possible. Charging for logging would starve the thing that eventually makes premium worth buying.

So: free users get to record everything. Premium users get insight, depth, and convenience.

Free

Unlimited restaurant & resort check-offs · unlimited star ratings · full catalog, search, and filters · list and map views · one active trip with countdown · percentage passport badges (per park and overall) · event check-offs · your own stats.

Premium

Visit notes, dishes ordered, and photos · unlimited trips and trip planning · complete passport including all challenge badges · live near-me map with walk-up/reservation filters · community Top 10 and trending (v1.1) · personalized recommendations (v1.2) · home-screen widget and Live Activity countdown · CSV and printable passport export · family trip sharing (v2).

Your #1 monetization risk

Seasonal churn. Someone subscribes the week of their trip, uses it hard for seven days, then has no reason to open the app for eleven months. Two defenses, and you need both: (a) the annual plan priced low enough that cancelling isn't worth the thought, and (b) the countdown widget on their home screen, which is the only feature that stays useful during the eleven-month gap. The widget is not a nice-to-have — it's your retention strategy.

Apple takes 30%, or 15% if you enroll in the Small Business Program (available to anyone under $1M/year — apply the day your developer account is active; it's free money). RevenueCat is free below $2,500/month in tracked revenue, then 1%.

Schema

Detail one — build for multiple destinations from day one

That destinations table costs you about twenty minutes now. Skip it, and adding Disneyland later means a migration that touches every table, every query, and every cached client. Disneyland roughly doubles your addressable market; Disney Cruise Line and Aulani are natural extensions after that. Every table hangs off destination_id from the first migration, even though there's exactly one row in it for the next year.

Detail two — "latest visit wins" needs a specific query

Your rule is that a venue's rating reflects only each user's most recent visit. That's a good rule, but the naive AVG(rating) gets it wrong by over-weighting frequent visitors. You want the average across users of each user's newest rating:

create view venue_latest_ratings as
  select distinct on (user_id, venue_id)
    user_id, venue_id, rating, would_return, visited_on
  from visits
  where rating is not null
  order by user_id, venue_id, visited_on desc, id desc;

create materialized view venue_stats as
  select venue_id,
         avg(rating)::numeric(3,2) as avg_rating,
         count(*) as rater_count,
         avg(would_return::int) as return_rate
  from venue_latest_ratings
  group by venue_id
  having count(*) >= 5;   -- hide thin data rather than show a lie

Refresh it hourly with pg_cron. The having clause matters: a Top 10 built on venues with two ratings each is worse than no Top 10, and it's the kind of thing that makes people cancel.

Detail three — achievements as data, not code

You have thirty-plus challenges and you'll invent more. Don't write thirty if statements. Give each achievement a JSON criteria blob and write one evaluator:

// "Eaten at 50% of Magic Kingdom restaurants"
{ "type": "venue_coverage", "scope": "area", "area_id": "mk", "threshold": 0.5 }

// "Drinking Around the World" — all 11 World Showcase pavilions
{ "type": "venue_set", "tag": "world_showcase_bar", "require": "all" }

// "Transportation Challenge" — 4 modes within one trip
{ "type": "trip_scoped_set", "field": "transport",
  "require": ["monorail","skyliner","bus","boat"], "window": "trip" }

// "4 Parks in One Day"
{ "type": "same_day_set", "field": "park", "count": 4 }

Five or six criteria types cover every challenge on your list. Adding "Snacking Around the World" then becomes a database row, not a deploy. Evaluate on-device for instant badge-unlock feedback, and re-verify server-side before anything touches a public leaderboard.

Execution

The build, phase by phase

Durations assume roughly 12 focused hours a week. Full-time, halve them. Phases are strictly ordered — each one's output is the next one's input.

1

Accounts & foundations

Week 1 · mostly waiting

Nothing here is hard, but Apple's approval is a hard blocker with a variable clock. Start it on day one, today, before you write any code.

  • Enroll in the Apple Developer Program ($99/yr). Individual enrollment approves in 24–48h and publishes under your legal name. Enrolling as an LLC requires a D-U-N-S number and can take 1–2 weeks — decide now, because changing later means a new listing.
  • Apply for the Small Business Program the moment you're approved — it drops Apple's cut from 30% to 15%.
  • Reserve the app name MagicalTracker in App Store Connect. Names are first-come and reserving is free.
  • Run a USPTO trademark search on "MagicalTracker" and book a 30-minute IP attorney consult (~$300–600). See Risks for what to ask.
  • Create: GitHub repo, Supabase project, Expo/EAS account, RevenueCat account, Sentry, PostHog.
  • npx create-expo-app, add NativeWind + Expo Router, get "Hello World" running on your physical iPhone via EAS Build. Do this before anything else — the first successful device build is where first-time app projects most often stall, and you want that pain on week one, not week nine.
2

Build the catalog

Weeks 2–3 · the long pole

This is unglamorous data entry and it is also your actual moat. Nobody has a clean, structured, geocoded WDW dining dataset — that's precisely why it's worth owning.

  • Assemble roughly 200 dining locations and ~30 resorts with: name, area, park/resort, service type, cuisine, price tier, reservations yes/no, character dining flag, lat/lng, official menu URL, and a one-or-two-sentence original description.
  • Write your own descriptions. Do not copy Disney's marketing text — that's a copyright issue distinct from trademark, and it's the easy one to avoid.
  • Geocode by dropping pins manually in Google Maps. Tedious, ~3 hours, and far more accurate than any geocoding API for in-park locations.
  • Tag venues for challenges as you go: world_showcase_bar, character_dining, signature, snack_stand. Tagging during entry costs minutes; retrofitting costs a day.
  • Seed achievements with your full challenge list and their criteria JSON.
  • Build the export script: Supabase → versioned JSON → bundled into the app.
Speed it up

Draft the first pass with an LLM to produce the rows, then verify every single one by hand against disneyworld.com. Models confidently invent restaurants that closed in 2019. A wrong menu link is a one-star review; a hallucinated restaurant is a credibility problem you can't undo.

3

The core loop

Weeks 4–6

Browse → open → log → see progress. When this loop feels good, you have an app. Everything after is amplification.

  • Auth: Sign in with Apple + email magic link, with a guest mode that lets someone log visits before creating an account. Forced signup on first launch is the single biggest install-to-active killer.
  • Catalog list with search, and filters for park, resort area, service type, reservations, and character dining.
  • Venue detail: description, facts, menu link in an in-app browser, your visit history, your rating.
  • Log-a-visit sheet: date, rating, "would you go back?", party size, meal period. Two taps from the venue list, always.
  • Resort stays — same pattern, smaller dataset.
  • Offline layer: seed SQLite on first launch, optimistic writes, mutation queue with replay on reconnect.
  • Profile: counts, coverage percentages, recent activity.
4

Passport, trips & map

Weeks 7–8

The features that turn a log into something people care about maintaining.

  • The achievement evaluator — one function, driven by the criteria JSON. Re-run on every visit write.
  • Passport screen: badge grid, locked/unlocked states, progress rings, and an unlock animation worth screenshotting.
  • Trips: create, assign visits to a trip, countdown screen.
  • Event check-offs for festivals and after-hours parties.
  • Map view: clustered pins, visited vs. unvisited styling, filter chips, tap-through to detail.
  • Home-screen widget + Live Activity for the countdown. Budget real time here — this is the trickiest Expo work in the whole project, and it's also your retention play.
5

Paywall & premium

Weeks 9–10

Wire up money. Mechanically simple, but full of small rules that cause rejections.

  • Create the three products in App Store Connect; wire RevenueCat entitlements; gate features off a single isPremium hook so the boundary lives in one place.
  • Build premium: visit notes, dishes, photos (Supabase Storage), unlimited trips, near-me map filters, CSV + printable export.
  • Paywall screen — trigger it contextually (when someone taps "add a note"), not as a launch interstitial.
  • Required by Apple: a visible Restore Purchases button, price and renewal terms shown before purchase, and links to your terms and privacy policy. Missing any of these is a guaranteed rejection.
  • Also required: in-app account deletion. Mandatory since 2022 for any app with account creation.
  • Ship the venue_stats views and Top 10 screen behind a feature flag, dark until the data justifies it.
6

Beta, polish & submit

Weeks 11–12

The gap between "works on my phone" and "approved" is mostly paperwork and empty states.

  • Onboarding: the rapid-fire "which of these have you been to?" pass (see Ideas) so the app is never empty on first open.
  • Every empty state, every loading state, every offline state. First-time apps are judged on these.
  • TestFlight with 15–25 real Disney-going testers — recruit from r/WaltDisneyWorld, Facebook planning groups, and DISboards. Their data corrections alone are worth the effort.
  • Ship MagicalTracker.com: landing page, privacy policy, terms, support page, and a support email. Apple blocks submission without live URLs.
  • App Store assets: icon, 6.7" and 6.1" screenshots, subtitle, keywords, description, age rating, and the Privacy Nutrition Label questionnaire.
  • Add the non-affiliation disclaimer to the app's About screen, the App Store description, and the website footer.
  • Submit. Expect 24–72h review, and expect at least one rejection — it's normal, not a verdict.

Attention required

Risks & concerns

Critical  Disney intellectual property

This is the risk that can end the project rather than merely delay it. The line to stay on is nominative fair use: naming a real place factually is protected, because there's no other way to refer to it. Evoking the brand is not.

Safe

Factual names of restaurants, resorts, parks, and events · your own descriptions · deep links to disneyworld.com · your own icon and artwork · a clear non-affiliation disclaimer.

Not safe

Disney logos, character art, or park photography you didn't shoot · the Mickey silhouette anywhere in the UI or icon · Disney's fonts (Waltograph especially) · copied menu or marketing text · trademarked slogans like "The Most Magical Place on Earth" · "Disney" in the app's name.

On the name itself: Disney holds a dense cluster of marks in this space — MagicBand, Magic Kingdom, My Magic+ — so "MagicalTracker" sits closer to that cluster than is comfortable. Using it is likely fine in practice; registering it as a trademark could draw an opposition. This is the specific question to bring to an attorney, along with your app description copy. Since you already own the domain, get the read before you build brand equity on it, not after.

Always include: "MagicalTracker is an independent app and is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or sponsored by The Walt Disney Company." In the app, in the App Store description, and in the website footer.

Critical  No My Disney Experience API

Covered above. Cut the feature; don't look for a clever workaround, because every workaround runs through someone's Disney password.

High  Catalog data goes stale

Disney restaurants open, close, rebrand, and change service models constantly. A dead menu link or a closed restaurant reads as abandonware and drives one-star reviews. Budget a recurring hour a month to reconcile against disneyworld.com, and ship a "report an issue" button on every venue detail screen — your users will maintain the dataset for you if you let them.

High  Seasonal churn

Detailed in Monetization. The countdown widget and the annual price are the answers. Track month-2 retention from launch day, because it's the number that tells you whether this is a business or a hobby.

High  Cold start on community features

Don't reveal a Top 10 until it's real. Gate it behind a feature flag and a minimum rater count. An empty leaderboard is worse than a missing one.

Medium  App Store review

Expect at least one rejection. The four most likely, in order: 4.2 minimum functionality (mitigated by shipping the map and passport, not just a checklist), 5.2 third-party IP (mitigated by the rules above), 3.1.1 in-app purchase compliance (restore button, visible terms), and 5.1.1 data collection — you must justify every permission you request and every data type on your privacy label.

Medium  Kids, privacy, and location

A Disney app will be used by and around children. If you rate the app 4+ or market to families, you inherit stricter obligations under COPPA and Apple's Kids Category rules — no behavioral advertising, no third-party tracking SDKs, tighter data handling. This is a second reason to defer family location sharing: it's the feature most likely to put a minor's real-time position in your database. When you do build it, make it opt-in per trip, foreground-only, and auto-expiring at the end of the trip.

Medium  Debugging native code from Windows

EAS Build handles compilation, but when a native module misbehaves you're reading cloud build logs instead of an Xcode stack trace. It's survivable and thousands of developers work this way. If the project gains traction, a used Mac mini (~$400–600) pays for itself quickly in reduced friction.

Medium  Reservation-availability alerts

Worth naming because it's the single highest-value feature you could build — Disney dining reservations are famously hard to get, and a drop alert would sell subscriptions on its own. It also requires polling Disney's systems, which is a terms-of-service violation and an infrastructure burden. Several apps do it and live in a grey zone. Know that it's the highest-risk, highest-reward item on the board, and don't put it in v1.

Recommendations

Ideas worth stealing

These aren't in your list, and several are worth more than features that are.

1. Offline-first is a headline feature, not a technical detail

Cell service in the parks is genuinely bad — crowded, concrete-heavy, and exactly where people want to log a meal. An app that works perfectly in a dead zone will get mentioned in reviews by name. This is the single highest-leverage architectural decision in the plan, which is why it's baked into phase 3.

2. Make it a scrapbook, not a checklist

A photo attached to every visit transforms what the app is. Checklists get abandoned; scrapbooks get revisited, and revisiting is retention. It also makes premium an easy sell, because photos are the feature people most want and least want to lose.

3. The end-of-trip recap card

When a trip's end date passes, generate a shareable image: "Jake's 2026 Trip — 14 restaurants, 3 new badges, 62% of Epcot complete." Disney fans post relentlessly in Facebook groups and on Reddit. This is your cheapest growth loop, it costs maybe two days to build, and every share carries your app name into an audience of exactly the right people.

4. Widgets and Live Activities carry the dead months

"142 days until your trip" on a home screen is the only thing keeping you present during the eleven months between visits. Named again here because it's the answer to your biggest monetization risk and it's easy to deprioritize as "polish."

5. Never let the app be empty on first launch

Onboarding should be a rapid tap-through: "Which of these have you eaten at?" across the 25 most popular restaurants. Thirty seconds later a new user has a populated passport, a coverage percentage, and probably a badge. Compare that to an empty grid and a "Log your first visit" button — the difference in day-two retention will be large.

6. Public profiles on MagicalTracker.com

A shareable web page at magicaltracker.com/u/jake showing someone's passport does three jobs at once: it's a share target for the recap card, it's SEO surface area, and it's social proof that the app has real users. You already build Next.js sites — this is a weekend, and it pairs directly with the SEO automation approach you've been planning.

7. "Would you go back?" beats stars for ranking

Star ratings inflate toward 4.5 and stop discriminating. A binary "would you go back?" is a cleaner, more honest signal and makes a far better Top 10 than average stars. Collect both — stars for the user's own memory, return-rate for your rankings. It's one extra column, and it's in the schema above.

8. A lifetime tier for the collector

The person who wants 100% passport completion is the same person who hates subscriptions and loves owning things. A one-time unlock converts a segment that would otherwise never pay you at all, with zero churn risk.

9. Plan the second destination into the first release

Not as a shipped feature — as a schema decision, already covered. Disneyland roughly doubles your market and, once the catalog pipeline exists, costs you a data-entry sprint rather than a rewrite.

Contrast check

White text clears WCAG AA on all six — #00807E is the lightest at about 4.8:1, which passes for body text but leaves no margin. Don't tint it lighter for hover states; go darker. Black text fails on all six, so treat the whole ramp as "white text only."

Three gaps to fill

  • A warm accent. The ramp has no warm tone, and achievement unlocks and premium moments both want gold — it's what a stamp, a medal, and a passport seal all look like. Add roughly #B07818 for light mode and #E5B45F for dark. It's the one color that should feel like a reward, and nothing in your current set can do that job.
  • Neutrals with a hue bias. Don't pair the ramp with pure greys — they'll read as cheap next to saturated teal. Bias your neutrals slightly cool: ground #F4F7F7, text #12212A, rules #D9E3E3. This page uses exactly those, so you can see them working under your accents.
  • Semantic colors. Success, warning, and error must be separate from your brand ramp, or a destructive confirm dialog ends up the same violet as the premium badge.

Dark mode

Your palette is already dark-native, which is the awkward part — on a dark ground these colors nearly vanish. Lighten the whole ramp 15–20% for dark mode rather than reusing it, exactly as this page does in its dark theme. Build both palettes as tokens from day one; retrofitting dark mode into a finished app is miserable, and iOS users expect it.

Start here today

Five things, in this order. The first two have waiting periods attached, so they go first regardless of what you feel like working on.

  1. Enroll in the Apple Developer Program. $99, 24–48h for individual approval. It blocks everything downstream and costs you nothing but time to start now. Decide individual vs. LLC before you click.
  2. Reserve "MagicalTracker" in App Store Connect the moment your account is live, and run a USPTO search on the name the same day.
  3. Get "Hello World" onto your iPhone via EAS Build. npx create-expo-app, add NativeWind and Expo Router, run a development build. Prove the Windows-to-iPhone pipeline works before you invest a single day in features — this is where first-time iOS projects most often die.
  4. Create the Supabase project and write the first Drizzle migration from the schema above. Include destinations, even with one row.
  5. Start the catalog. Twenty restaurants a night for two weeks. It's the least fun part of the project and the part nobody else has, and beginning it early means it's finished when you need it rather than blocking you in week six.
